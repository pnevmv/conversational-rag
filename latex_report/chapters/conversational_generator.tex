\chapter{Conversational Generator and Generator Models}\label{ch:convgen}

In all systems of the previous chapters the generator sees only the current user turn
and the retrieved context. The dialogue history reaches only the query rewriter, and the
rewritten query is used only for retrieval, so a follow-up such as ``and how do I renew
it?'' is answered without knowing what ``it'' refers to, unless the retrieved sections
make it clear. The history given to the rewriter is the gold one (the reference agent
turns of the dataset, not the system's own answers), which is the standard turn-level
protocol of \gls{mdd}. This chapter gives the generator the dialogue, studies how the
decision to abstain should then be taken, and compares three generator models on the
resulting system. All experiments use the full validation split only; the test split was
not used. Retrieval, the graph facts and the contexts are the same as in
Chapter~\ref{ch:final}; only the end of the generation prompt and the generator model
change.

\section{What the Generator Sees}\label{sec:convgen:input}

Four generator inputs were compared, for the passage pipeline and for passages with graph
facts (E):
\begin{description}
    \item[G0] the current user turn, as in all previous chapters;
    \item[G1] the rewritten query instead of the user turn;
    \item[G2] the gold dialogue history, under a ``Conversation so far'' header, followed by
        the user turn;
    \item[G3] G2 plus the rewritten query after the user turn.
\end{description}
On first turns there is no history and the rewrite equals the user turn, so all four
inputs give the same prompt. The length of the history in G2 was chosen on dev-500 among
2, 4 and all turns; the mean \fone{} of the two pipelines was 28.8, 29.0 and 29.1, and the
whole history was kept. In G2 and G3 the generator therefore sees every earlier user and
agent turn of the dialogue.

Table~\ref{tab:convgen:input} shows the results. The rewritten query in the prompt makes
the answers worse, alone (G1) or added to the history (G3). The history alone (G2) gains
on answerable questions, $+0.8$ points (95\% interval $[0.5, 1.1]$) with passages and
$+0.7$ ($[0.4, 1.0]$) with passages and facts, but the model abstains about half as often,
so most of the gain is lost on the unanswerable questions and the overall difference is
not significant. The gain is largest where the user turn cannot be understood alone: on
replies of the form ``yes'' to a question of the agent (531 turns) G2 gains 3.7 points with
passages and 3.2 with facts, and on turns where the reference agent asks a clarifying
question it gains 0.9 and 0.7 points. On negative replies (``no'', ``do not'', 488 turns)
it loses 2.8 and 2.4 points.

The G0 numbers differ slightly from Table~\ref{tab:final:val} (26.8 against 26.9 for the
passage pipeline). In the original run, identical prompts sent at the same time sometimes
received different answers and only one of them was cached; here every input uses the
cached answer, which changes 17 answers of the passage pipeline and 13 of E.

\section{The Abstention Decision}\label{sec:convgen:abstain}

In the unanswerable turns where G0 abstains and G2 answers (33 with passages, 26 with
facts), the user turn is always a negative reply to a condition question of the agent,
for example ``do not'' after ``Have you moved to New York State and become a resident?''.
The dataset expects a refusal, while G2 continues the conversation, sometimes with an
answer that is reasonable for the dialogue (``If you haven't become a New York State
resident, you don't need to exchange your out-of-state driver license''). Deciding
whether to answer and writing the answer are therefore two different problems.

Taking the decision from G0 and the answer from G2 (G2 + abstain(G0): the refusal when G0
abstains, the G2 answer otherwise) recovers the unanswerable turns and gives the best
overall \fone{}: 27.5 with passages and 28.0 with passages and facts, $+0.6$ and $+0.7$
points over G0 (Table~\ref{tab:convgen:input}). It needs two generation calls for most
turns, which roughly doubles the input tokens of the generator.

Two cheaper decisions were tried with the same generator. The first is a short call that
sees the retrieved context, the history and the current turn and answers only ``yes'' or
``no'' (reasoning disabled, about five output tokens). It says no on about 20\% of the
turns: it catches half of the unanswerable questions but refuses 18 to 20\% of the
answerable ones, and loses 1.3 points with passages and 1.0 with facts against
G2 + abstain(G0). The second is a threshold on the score of the top section given by the
cross-encoder, chosen on dev-500 (0.303). It abstains on only 3\% of the turns and loses
0.6 points in both pipelines: the reranker usually finds a relevant-looking section even
when the answer is not in it. G2 + abstain(G0) is therefore kept as the conversational
system.

\section{Token Usage}\label{sec:convgen:tokens}

The usage reported by the API was not stored in the first experiments: the client kept
only the answer text, and the cost estimates of the earlier systems were obtained by
counting the tokens of the stored prompts and answers. The client was then changed to
record, for every call, the input tokens, the cached input tokens, the output tokens and
the hidden reasoning tokens, together with the model, the call type (rewrite, G0 or G2),
the conversation and the turn. The cache stores the usage with the answer, so a cached
call is logged with the usage of the original call and marked as cached. All numbers in
the next section come from this log.

For the whole project the Azure bill of the \texttt{gpt-5.4-nano} deployment is EUR 34.56
for 164.62 million input and 11.56 million output tokens, which includes the graph
construction and all runs before the comparison of the next section.

\section{Generator Models}\label{sec:convgen:models}

Three generator models available on the same Azure endpoint were compared on the system
G2 + abstain(G0): \texttt{gpt-5.4-nano}, used in all previous chapters, \texttt{gpt-5-nano}
and \texttt{gpt-6-luna}. Only the two generation calls change model; the query rewriter
stays on \texttt{gpt-5.4-nano}, and the prompts are exactly those of
Section~\ref{sec:convgen:input}. The three models were run fresh on the full validation
split with the default reasoning settings. A second run of \texttt{gpt-5.4-nano} is used as
the reference, so that all three runs use the same code; it differs from the cached run of
Table~\ref{tab:convgen:input} by at most 0.1 points of \fone{} on all questions, which
gives an idea of the variation between two runs of the same model.

Costs use the USD list prices of the Azure Retail Prices API for a Global Standard
deployment in the East US 2 region, checked on 21 October 2026 (input / cached input /
output per million tokens): \texttt{gpt-5.4-nano} 0.20 / 0.02 / 1.25, \texttt{gpt-5-nano}
0.05 / 0.005 / 0.40 and \texttt{gpt-6-luna} 0.10 / 0.01 / 0.50 (short-context meters). The
deployment type of the project's deployments is not confirmed, so the real prices can
differ. The project's tokens at the \texttt{gpt-5.4-nano} list price would cost between USD
17.74 (all input cached) and USD 47.37 (no input cached), a range that contains the billed
EUR 34.56.

Table~\ref{tab:convgen:models} shows that neither newer model improves the system. On
answerable questions both are worse than \texttt{gpt-5.4-nano}, by 1.0 to 1.8 points; on
all questions \texttt{gpt-5-nano} ties and \texttt{gpt-6-luna} loses 0.8 points. The two newer
models abstain much more often: \texttt{gpt-5-nano} catches about half of the unanswerable
questions but refuses 12\% of the answerable ones, against 4 to 6\% for
\texttt{gpt-5.4-nano}, and \texttt{gpt-6-luna} refuses 14 to 17\%.

The loss comes from these refusals and not from worse answers. On the answerable
questions where all three models answer (3{,}326 with passages, 3{,}443 with facts), the
three models reach the same \fone{} within noise (30.7, 31.0 and 31.1 with passages,
30.8, 30.7 and 30.8 with facts). The newer models write shorter answers, 34 to 41 words on
average against 46 to 48 for \texttt{gpt-5.4-nano}. Taking the abstention decision from
\texttt{gpt-5.4-nano} and only the answer from a newer model does not help either: on the
questions where the cached answers make this combination possible, it differs from
\texttt{gpt-5.4-nano} alone by at most 0.4 points, with no significant difference.

The token usage differs mostly in the output. \texttt{gpt-5.4-nano} used no reasoning
tokens with the default settings. \texttt{gpt-5-nano} spends about 1{,}430 hidden reasoning
tokens per turn, about ten times its visible answer, so despite the lowest price per token
it costs more than \texttt{gpt-5.4-nano} with passages and only somewhat less with facts.
\texttt{gpt-6-luna} reasons moderately (80 to 100 tokens per turn) and is the cheapest,
about half the cost of \texttt{gpt-5.4-nano}. A conversation of the validation split (6.7
turns on average) uses about 16{,}700 input tokens with passages and 38{,}800 with facts
(95th percentile 27{,}300 and 55{,}400). Prompt caching saves little on a normal run, since
only 7 to 19\% of the input is served from the cache. \texttt{gpt-5.4-nano} was kept as the
generator.
